Why lamps make this easier

The constraint is an advantage

Most smart-lighting guides assume you are replacing a ceiling fixture or a wall switch, which means breakers, landlords, and a real chance of doing it wrong. You have none of that. Lamps plug into a socket and take a standard bulb, so the entire lighting layer is screw in a bulb, done.

You also get something the overhead-light crowd does not: lamps are already the warm, low, pooled light that people spend money trying to fake. Once they are on a dimmer that knows what time it is, the room does the thing you actually want automatically — bright and neutral at 2pm, twenty percent and candle-warm at midnight.

The shape of it

Three layers, in this order

Almost everyone starts by buying a gadget and ends up with four apps that cannot talk to each other. Build the layers in order instead.

1

A brain

Home Assistant. Free, open source, runs entirely on your own hardware — no subscription, no cloud account, and it keeps working when the internet does not. This is the piece that makes every other brand cooperate.

~$60 · used mini PC, or $0 if you have an old machine
2

A radio

Zigbee, via a USB stick in the brain. Battery sensors last a year or two instead of a week, devices cost a fraction of the Wi-Fi equivalents, and nothing routes through a company's server.

~$30 · dongle plus one cable you must not skip
3

The things themselves

A door sensor and two bulbs. That is the entire first build. Presence sensing and a voice puck come later — they are better purchases once you know how you actually move through the room.

~$39 · sensor plus two tunable-white bulbs

Start tonight for nothing

Before you spend a cent

Install Home Assistant on your Windows machine — Docker or a VirtualBox VM, both are documented on the Home Assistant installation page. It will not control anything yet, but you will spend an hour inside the UI, build a fake automation, and find out whether you like working in it. That is a free answer to the only question that matters before buying hardware.

Do not keep it there long term, though. Your PC sleeps and reboots, and a room whose lights depend on that is worse than a room with an ordinary light switch.

Check your lamps before ordering bulbs. Two things can rule a lamp out: an integrated LED with no removable bulb, or an unusual base. Match the base to where you are — E26 in the US, E27 across most of Europe and Asia, B22 bayonet in the UK and parts of the Commonwealth.

If a lamp fails either test, use a Zigbee smart plug instead, around $12. You lose dimming and colour temperature, since it is on/off only, but the door automation works exactly the same.

Wiring it up

Saturday afternoon, in order

  1. Flash Home Assistant OS to the mini PC's drive and boot from it. Bare metal, not Windows underneath — it is a purpose-built appliance OS and it handles its own updates.
  2. Open homeassistant.local:8123 from your PC, create an account, and set your location so sunset times are correct.
  3. Plug the Zigbee dongle in via the extension cable. Home Assistant will discover it and offer to set up ZHA. Accept.
  4. Pair the two bulbs first. Mains-powered Zigbee devices act as repeaters, so they build the mesh that the battery door sensor will lean on.
  5. Pair the door sensor, then stick it on. The magnet and the body need to sit within roughly 15 mm of each other when the door is shut.
  6. Make a group: Settings → Devices & Services → Helpers → Create Helper → Group → Light group. Put both bulbs in it and call it Bedroom Lamps. Every automation below targets the group, so adding a third lamp later needs no edits.

The automations

Paste into the YAML editor, swap the entity names

In each automation editor, use the three-dot menu → Edit in YAML. Replace binary_sensor.bedroom_door_contact, light.bedroom_lamps, and person.mark with whatever yours are actually called.

1 · Door opens → lamps on, at the right warmth

alias: Door opens - lamps on
description: Light the room on entry. Dim and candle-warm after 22:00.
triggers:
  - trigger: state
    entity_id: binary_sensor.bedroom_door_contact
    to: "on"          # "on" means open
conditions:
  - condition: state
    entity_id: light.bedroom_lamps
    state: "off"         # don't stomp on lights you set by hand
actions:
  - action: light.turn_on
    target:
      entity_id: light.bedroom_lamps
    data:
      brightness_pct: "{{ (20 if now().hour >= 22 or now().hour < 7 else 75) | int }}"
      kelvin: "{{ (2200 if now().hour >= 22 or now().hour < 7 else 3500) | int }}"
      transition: 1
mode: single

2 · Phone leaves home → everything off

alias: I left home - lamps off
description: Free presence detection, no extra hardware.
triggers:
  - trigger: state
    entity_id: person.mark
    from: home
    to: not_home
actions:
  - action: light.turn_off
    target:
      entity_id: light.bedroom_lamps
mode: single

This one needs the free Home Assistant companion app on your phone, with location permission granted. That app is also your voice control for now — it has an assistant built in, so you get "turn on the lamps" from day one without buying a speaker.

3 · Sunset → the room warms down on its own

alias: Sunset warm-down
description: Half an hour before sunset, ease into evening light.
triggers:
  - trigger: sun
    event: sunset
    offset: "-00:30:00"
conditions:
  - condition: state
    entity_id: light.bedroom_lamps
    state: "on"
actions:
  - action: light.turn_on
    target:
      entity_id: light.bedroom_lamps
    data:
      brightness_pct: 45
      kelvin: 2700
      transition: 300   # five-minute fade, so you never catch it happening
mode: single

Once you are comfortable, replace this third one with the Adaptive Lighting custom integration, installed through HACS. It shifts colour temperature continuously across the whole day rather than in one step, and for a room lit entirely by lamps it is the best free upgrade available.

Things that will bite you

Every one of these is a real evening lost

USB 3 murders Zigbee

USB 3 ports, and the SSDs plugged into them, leak noise across the same 2.4 GHz band Zigbee uses. A dongle plugged directly into the back of a PC often ends up with a range of about one metre. The $6 extension cable, with the dongle hanging in open air, is the fix — and it is why that cable is on the parts list rather than an afterthought.

The lamp's own switch has to stay on

A smart bulb with its power cut is just a bulb. Get in the habit of leaving lamp switches on and controlling everything through Home Assistant. If a switch is easy to knock by accident, tape it. A cheap Zigbee button on the nightstand scratches the "I want to press something" itch properly.

Do not auto-off on a timer

The obvious next automation is "lights off ten minutes after the door closes." Do not build it. It turns the lights off on you while you are sitting perfectly still at your desk, and it is the most common reason people rip this stuff back out. Turning off when you leave home is safe; turning off on stillness needs a presence sensor, which is phase two.

Battery sensors look broken when they are fine

A door sensor reports only when its state changes, so "last updated" can read as hours ago. That is correct behaviour, not a dying battery.

What comes after

Once phase one has run for a week

4

Presence, so the lights stop being wrong

An mmWave presence sensor — Everything Presence One, or an Aqara FP2 — detects breathing-level motion, so it knows you are in the room even when you have not moved in twenty minutes. This is the upgrade that turns "clever" into "invisible", and it is the right thing to buy next.

~$50
5

An actual wake word

Home Assistant Voice Preview Edition is a purpose-built mic-and-speaker puck that runs the whole pipeline locally: wake word, speech to text, intent, speech back. The wake word is a configuration field, so yes — it can genuinely be "Jarvis".

~$59
6

The part that makes it feel like Jarvis

Out of the box, voice control is a spoken remote: it understands "turn on the desk lamp" and very little else. Home Assistant lets you set an LLM as the conversation agent, and ships an Anthropic integration for exactly this. Then "it's a bit dim in here" and "I'm heading to bed" work, because something is interpreting intent rather than matching phrases. That gap is the whole difference between a voice remote and an assistant.

No hardware · API usage billed per request
7

Light as a material

An ESP32 running WLED driving an addressable LED strip costs around $25 all in, and comprehensively beats any off-the-shelf strip on effects and control. Behind the desk, under the bed frame, along a shelf — this is where a lamp-lit room becomes genuinely yours.

~$25

Build phase one, then live with it for a full week before buying anything else. You will find that one automation you were certain you wanted is quietly annoying, and that something you never considered is the thing you use twenty times a day. That week is worth more than any parts list.
